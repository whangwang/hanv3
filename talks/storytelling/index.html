How to Do Storytelling.

You already design for users. Here's how to design for the stakeholders you've been ignoring.

A scrolling companion to my 45–60 minute talk on stakeholder storytelling for designers. Read at your pace, drop a comment on any line, and the threads will live on after the room empties.

Open cold: a room of 600.

30k+Field agents
600+In the pitch room
1Failed transformation prior

Most of the workflow lived in paper notes, personal spreadsheets, screenshots of policy details, and chat threads with the back office. None of it standard. All of it working — for them.

On top of that: hard regulatory walls, a security review at every turn, and a failed earlier digital transformation that had bruised agent trust and shaken morale.

The brief from the client was, in essence, one word: modernise.

I could have walked in with a beautiful redesign. It would have failed.
Here's what we did instead.

04

What storytelling actually is.

Quickly, since you already do this for users — the difference between describing and telling:

Describe

"A waterproof shoe in a nice green."

Tell

"A shoe built for climbers — waterproof for the ascent, coloured to sit in the landscape."

Same shoe. The second one earns attention because it carries tension (the climb) and a stake (does this shoe survive it?). For stakeholders, this is the move:

A stakeholder story isn't context.
It's tension plus a decision the audience has to make.
End every story on a decision.

One honesty beat: storytelling organises truth for comprehension. It does not bend it. The frame is yours to choose; the facts aren't.

05

Stakeholders are users.

You'd never ship a product without knowing the user. So why present to stakeholders you've never researched?

Their context window is tiny — thirty minutes to judge months of work. Their visual taste is personal, not market-fit. They often can't name what's wrong; they just know the room's quiet.

So treat the presentation itself as an experience-design problem. Who's the audience? What's their journey through your deck? What are their pains? Where's the affordance to engage?

That reframe gives us three design challenges — and they structure the rest of this talk:

  • Support the decision
  • Lower the load
  • Raise engagement

We didn't call the pitch a "design review." We named it a public hearing. The naming did design work — it told the agents they'd be heard.

06

Support the decision.

Propose strategies, not designs.
Let stakeholders choose between strategies, not pixels.

▶ Shown live: two homepage concepts, each a different strategy, put to the room as a live vote. Not "do you prefer this blue?" but "which world do we want to build?"

Wrap design in evidence.

Tie every move to a goal they already care about. Two examples from the AG App pitch:

  • Faster login→ "make the time on the road count"
  • Quote on the spot→ "capture the moment, shorten the sales cycle"

When someone says "I don't like it but I can't say why."

That's not rejection. It's an unmet requirement they haven't articulated yet. Re-route taste into a requirement with one question:

"What would this need to do for you to feel confident?"

Always have a Plan B they'll like.

Bring the safer version. It shows you understand feasibility, and it gives the room somewhere to land if Plan A is too much.

The nuance: if Plan B is always the safe one, you train them to always pick safe. The frame I use — Plan B earns you the right to be bold by proving you understand the constraints; Plan A is what we'd do if those constraints were softer than they assumed.

07

Make it appealing.

You design for users. Now design for the people reading your deck. One idea per slide. Visible hierarchy. Low cognitive load. The artifact is part of the argument.

What makes the deck yours and not a flat double-diamond recital: the real photos, the real quotes, the one unique finding nobody else would have noticed. The finding from the AG App pitch — the one that earned the room — was this:

Agents don't think by task type — they think by customer. Every workaround they'd built was a way to cluster work back around the person they were about to call.

That sentence carried the entire homepage strategy. It came from shadowing, not a survey.

Voice and tone.

Positive but not evasive. "90% success rate," not "10% chance of failure." Same fact, different stake. The frame you pick changes the decision the room makes — so pick it deliberately.

The caveat: spin gets smelled. If the safer frame is also the dishonest one, drop it. Trust takes one presentation to lose and three to rebuild.

08

Raise engagement.

Don't describe engagement. Design affordances for it into the presentation itself.

▶ Shown live: the Q&A wall. 600+ agents typed questions in real time — and upvoted each other's — so the best questions rose to the top. The wall did the moderating.

The site you're reading right now uses the exact same mechanic. Select any line. Add a comment. Upvote.

And then: answer the room section by section, not all saved to the end. That way they feel heard rather than lectured. The lecture format makes Q&A feel like an exam at the end of a long class; the section format makes it feel like a conversation.

09

The storytelling canvas.

Six fields. Fill them out before the next time you stand up to present. If any of them are empty, you don't have a story yet — you have a status update.

Nothing here is saved or sent anywhere. Copy what you've written somewhere you'll find it again.

Design for your stakeholders the way you design for your users, and they'll let you do your best work.
